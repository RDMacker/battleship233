the real problem

it is not that they cannot get to the video

most hockey video tools solve storage. they give you more footage, faster, better organised. but ask an alumni group what video sessions felt like and you hear the same thing, that they sat in a room watching game film without knowing what they were supposed to be looking at. players do not skip video because it is hard to find. they skip it because it feels like work with no payoff.

coaches have the mirror image of that problem. you know exactly what each athlete needs to fix, and no realistic way to sit down with twelve of them individually every week.

players see it as homework.

footage without a specific question attached is just more screen time.

coaches have no hours to give.

individual sessions do not scale past a handful of athletes.

the good stuff gets lost.

clips live in shared drives and text threads, and nobody goes back to them.

how it works

thirty seconds in, one clear thing out

1

the athlete films.

phone on the boards, parent in the stands, or a clip pulled from game footage. thirty seconds is enough.

2

it lands in your feed.

organised by athlete, not by date, so you are never hunting through a folder.

3

you mark it up and ask one question.

draw the line, slow it down, and attach the thing you want them to notice. "watch your inside edge on the first crossover, what happens to your hips?"

4

they answer, and it is logged.

the exchange sits on their profile, so in six weeks you can put week one next to week six and show them the change.

the whole thing takes a coach about four minutes per athlete, and it happens whenever you have four minutes.

reference clips

agree on what good looks like

ask ten coaches in a room what effort looks like and you get ten answers, until somebody puts a clip on the screen. the same is true of a clean crossover, a proper shooting base, or a goalie who is square. words are slow and clips are instant.

sportsense lets you build your own reference clips, the reps you want every athlete measuring themselves against, and hold them next to an athlete's own footage. because they are your clips, they stay current, and they say what you actually mean.
