# Content Think Tank: sportsense — 2026-11-17

The niche enters this report's window in full off-season mode, and the two stories that matter most to sportsense are economic and structural. The bike industry crisis deepened in early November, with Fox Factory's Q3 sales down 10.1 percent, Bianchi entering restructuring around November 3 to 5, and German brand Ghost reported closed after 33 years on November 5, which makes service-business economics unusually salient to every coach reading the trade press. At the same moment the between-visits gap just opened on schedule: NICA's fall regionals wrapped November 14 and 15, athletes are going quiet until spring, and TrainingPeaks, REI, Singletracks and Enduro MTB Training are all telling riders winter is the window for skills work without explaining where feedback comes from. The platforms are rewarding exactly what a founder-led coaching brand can make cheaply, since YouTube's October 2026 Shorts update suppresses reuploads in favour of creator-voiced analysis, Instagram's confirmed ranking signals are watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach, and carousels are earning roughly nine times the saves of single images in 2026 benchmark data. On the audience side, remote coaching's live objections are logistical rather than philosophical (the Kasper Woolley launch comments were about filming angles, not whether it works), riders are openly mocking AI that pretends to coach after the Strava Athlete Intelligence backlash, and with organic CTR on AI Overview queries down to roughly 0.61 percent, the blog pays off only as a citation asset.

## The slate

### Blog

**Your certification is not your credential, your footage is** (essay, boldness 4)
A stake-in-the-ground essay written straight into the live Pinkbike argument where the top comment on the Level 4 announcement called the certification obsession out of hand. The position only this brand can hold is that the one proof a skeptical client can evaluate is documented athlete change, meaning before and after video of the same rider on the same feature with a visible mechanical difference, and the piece walks through what a progression archive looks like in practice. Structured for AEO with question-phrased headings and direct 40 to 60 word answers, with sportsense appearing only at the end as the infrastructure that makes keeping the library trivial. Merged with insider-3's carousel pitch of the same thesis, which becomes the feed distribution cut produced in the same sitting.
Hook: the most upvoted comment on the biggest coaching certification announcement of the year says the certification obsession is getting out of hand, and the uncomfortable part is that the commenter is mostly right.
Why now: the Level 4 backlash is the most upvoted credibility argument the coaching audience has had in months, and concurrent Singletrack threads show riders actively searching for a better quality signal.
Critics: unanimous advance at 7.7, called a genuine stake in the ground that doubles as the canonical cited answer to how do I know if a coach is good.

**Riders are right to laugh at the AI coach** (question-led AEO post with a Reel cut, boldness 4)
Opens by agreeing with the Strava power users who called Athlete Intelligence the latest gimmick in the AI explosion, then writes down the distinction the audience already makes but nobody has published: software that pretends to coach deserves the ridicule, while tooling that extends a human coach's eye across distance and time is a different category. The overlay is framed as telemetry a coach reads, not a verdict a machine issues, and the same argument gets condensed into a founder-voiced Reel (absorbing thought-leader-3's demonstration beat of naming the judgment call only a coach can make) and seeded as disclosed answers in the Reddit threads where the mockery lives.
Hook: can AI replace a mountain bike skills coach, answered immediately with no, and the riders mocking AI coaching apps have correctly identified why, because feedback without a trained eye behind it is noise.
Why now: the Strava backlash is the audience's current reference point for AI coaching, and no practitioner voice has claimed this distinction while small-site CTR sits near 0.61 percent on affected queries.
Critics: advanced by all three at 6.7, named the canonical home for the argument on condition the duplicates were cut, with a note to verify the Strava anecdotes before quoting them.

**What should a coach charge for remote video analysis** (AEO citation post, boldness 1)
A question heading with a direct 40 to 60 word answer beneath it, then the real math built from the coach's income problem rather than software features, using current instructor pay data of roughly 21 to 24 dollars an hour on seasonal postings, a comparison table of session pricing against monthly package pricing, and a worked ten-athlete roster example. The point of view is that the hourly session rate is the wrong anchor because the product is continuity between sessions. It becomes the answer Rich can also give, disclosed, in coaching subreddits.
Hook: most coaches price remote video analysis against their hourly session rate, which is the wrong anchor, because the product is continuity between sessions, and a monthly price well below a session converts better and retains longer.
Why now: the blog only pays as a citation asset in the current search environment, and nobody in the niche owns this answer.
Critics: unanimous advance at 7.0, honest about its job as a citation asset, with the ZipRecruiter figures to be verified before publishing.

### YouTube

**How to film yourself so a coach can actually see something** (Short, cross-posted as a Reel, boldness 2)
Answers the single most repeated objection in the Woolley online coaching comments, that remote analysis seems tough to pull off because someone has to film you from the right angles. Rich shows the honest minimum of one phone, one angle and ten seconds of trail, then demonstrates on screen what a trained eye and a skeletal overlay extract from exactly that clip, including what a bad angle hides. Merged with analyst-1's near-identical pitch, which survives as the save-oriented companion carousel written in the literal phrases riders search, so the pair gets both the fast Reel spike and the slow carousel tail.
Hook: opening frame of a deliberately useless clip with text reading that this is how most riders film themselves, and no coach on earth can tell you anything from it.
Why now: the filming question is the live, unanswered doorway objection to the whole category, and the October Shorts originality update rewards exactly this founder-voiced format.
Critics: unanimous advance at 7.7, the stronger of the two filming pitches because showing what a bad angle hides is itself the demonstration of the trained eye.

**What the overlay missed, coach's eye versus the app** (Shorts series, boldness 3)
Each episode runs one rider clip through the kind of automated form-check output consumer AI apps produce, shows what the automated read says, then Rich points at what the numbers cannot see, the timing decision, the terrain read, the commitment point two seconds before the fault. Merged with maverick-5, keeping that pitch's cumulative scoreboard and its commitment to scoring honestly when the app catches something real, because the argument is that the tool's job is to extend a coach's eye, not that software is useless. Which real app output appears on screen gets settled before episode one, since naming competitors carries risk.
Hook: the app says this rider's knee angle is fine, and the app is right, which is exactly why she keeps going over the bars.
Why now: riders currently distrust AI coaches but expect video feedback, which is precisely the gap, and the Shorts update favours this construction over anything templated.
Critics: the strongest execution of four AI-versus-coach pitches, averaging 7.0, praised for conceding the app's point before beating it, with the best single opening line anyone pitched.

### Instagram

**The clip review** (weekly submitted-footage Reels series, flagship, boldness 3)
The merge of the slate's three clip-review pitches into one show, built on producer-1's operational spine (fixed learnable episode structure, Stories submission loop, Edits in-video links chaining episodes) with maverick-1's deduction mechanic as the engine: Rich cold-reads a ten second clip with the overlay running, talks through what the hips, elbows and timing tell him, and commits on camera to a conclusion before the reveal. Per the operator's fix, the reveal is verifiable (the problem the rider says they have) rather than an unverifiable coaching-hours guess, and the first month is seeded from existing coaching footage with athlete permission rather than relying on cold-start submissions. Every episode demonstrates the two-way feedback loop without ever being a demo.
Hook: on-screen text over the frozen first frame reading that this rider swears the problem is the jump, and Rich is going to show you in ten seconds of video why it is actually the corner before it.
Why now: chess creator culture proved experts reviewing amateur material mints audiences and sells disproportionately well, nobody in MTB runs the format, and both platforms' originality updates reward it.
Critics: the room's consensus flagship, with the guess mechanic rated best idea on the slate by two critics and the production spec rated best by all three.

**This winter's skills advice is teaching riders to rehearse their mistakes** (carousel, boldness 4)
A direct critique of the off-season content wave, naming the blind spot in every winter-skills article currently running: none explains where feedback comes from between now and spring. The mechanism claim, kept defensible per the critics, is that unobserved repetition consolidates whatever you already do, so a rider drilling cornering alone for four months is often entrenching the fault a coach spent a session correcting. One mechanism per slide, OCR-searchable slide text, and a send trigger aimed at the riding partner doing solo winter drills.
Hook: every training outlet is telling you winter is for skills work, and almost none of them will tell you the awkward part, which is that four months of unwatched practice mostly makes your current mistakes more permanent.
Why now: off-season training content is the niche's dominant editorial theme right now and athletes went quiet the week NICA regionals ended.
Critics: unanimous advance at 7.7, a genuinely contrarian position none of the outlets being critiqued can take, provided the motor-learning claim stays stated as consolidation rather than overstated.

**The message every coach knows** (carousel series, boldness 5)
Text-message-screenshot storytelling applied to coach churn: a rendered chat thread of the one conversation every coach has lived, an athlete who loves training with them but cannot afford to come more often, the warm helpless reply, the silence, then a data slide pricing that silence as a four figure hole in the year. The final slides replay the same thread with a different reply offering a monthly remote option in plain words, and per the audience advocate the chats are clearly presented as dramatisation, not real messages, to protect trust with a skeptical coach audience.
Hook: slide one is a phone screenshot of an incoming message reading that honestly I love training with you but I just cannot afford to come more often, with a caption beginning that every coach has this exact thread in their phone and most of them replied the expensive way.
Why now: the off-season disappearance is happening right now, and current retention writing frames churn as a visibility problem, which this thread makes literal.
Critics: unanimous advance at 7.3, the best message-to-effort ratio among coach-facing ideas, landing the brand's founding line inside the platform's most shareable container.

**Nobody wants to be someone's £400 regret** (Reel with companion carousel, boldness 3)
Built from the live Singletrack thread where a rider paid north of £400 to basically stand still. Rich reads the complaint on camera, takes the rider's side rather than defending coaches, then walks through what separates a session an athlete remembers from one they half-forget: mechanism-specific feedback, a record the athlete can rewatch, and a follow-up that builds on it, with the framing kept as arming coaches rather than accusing them. The product stays out of frame until the caption.
Hook: on-screen text over real analysis footage reading that a rider on a UK forum just said they paid over £400 for coaching and stood still, and the uncomfortable part is that they are probably not lying.
Why now: the thread is still anchoring the coaching credibility fight, and the off-season is when coaches decide what their 2027 offer looks like.
Critics: unanimous advance at 7.3, with taking the complaining rider's side against his own customer base named the scroll-stopping move that earns coach trust.

**The fault file** (Reels series engineered to be sent, boldness 2)
Each episode isolates one instantly recognisable riding fault (heels up on steeps, inside shoulder dropping, braking into the lip), shows the mechanism with a side-by-side overlay comparison, and ends with a spoken and on-screen instruction to send it to the riding partner who does this. The finite catalogue of fifteen to twenty named faults gives it an episode engine, captions are written in the literal search phrases riders type, and analyst-2's duplicate pitch is absorbed here. The send close is the distribution mechanism, built against the confirmed sends-per-reach signal rather than bolted on.
Hook: opening text overlay reading that your riding partner drops their heels on every descent except the one where it would save them, and from the side it looks exactly like this.
Why now: sends now weigh slightly more than likes for non-follower reach, which makes a scripted send trigger the cheapest growth mechanic available to a small coaching account.
Critics: unanimous advance at 7.3, the workhorse series of the slate, a system rather than a gimmick because of the named catalogue and search-phrase captions.

**The open kitchen** (weekly timestamped process Reel, boldness 3)
Rich films his actual remote review workflow exactly as it happens, timestamps carrying the narrative: the clip arrives, the overlay goes on, the fault gets found, a voice note goes back, elapsed time on screen at the end. It answers the live logistical disbelief that virtual coaching seems tough to pull off by showing the whole transaction taking seventeen minutes, and for coaches watching it quietly shows what earning between sessions physically looks like. The only gate is explicit athlete consent to show their clip and feedback on camera, and the trend-report citation gets verified or cut, since the idea stands without it.
Hook: timestamp overlay on the first frame reading that a clip arrived at 9:14 this morning, and by 9:31 the athlete had feedback she can rewatch every day this winter.
Why now: process-heavy, unpolished founder footage is currently outperforming polished content, and the work is already happening weekly, so the camera just attends it.
Critics: advanced at 7.3, called the highest payoff-per-effort item on the slate because it answers the objection with evidence rather than claims at near-zero cost.

### Email

**Stop treating remote coaching as the discount version of you** (newsletter essay, boldness 4)
The highest-scored idea in the room. It attacks the belief coaches themselves hold most deeply, that in-person is the real product and remote is a compromise they should feel embarrassed to charge for, using evidence they cannot dismiss: roughly half of personal trainers now run hybrid in-person plus online as their primary model, esports normalised paid asynchronous VOD review years ago, and the objections to the Woolley launch were about filming, not the concept. It closes on retention (the athlete who hears from their coach between visits is the athlete still paying in March) with one CTA, a worked pricing example for a first remote package.
Hook: subject line, the part of coaching you are giving away free. Opening sentence, every coach I know treats the weeks between sessions as dead time they are not allowed to charge for, and half the personal training industry quietly stopped believing that years ago.
Why now: this is the month coaches feel the between-visits revenue gap most sharply, and the hybrid training data plus the Woolley discourse give the argument current ammunition.
Critics: unanimous advance at 8.0, praised for attacking the belief the audience actually holds, with the subject line called the strongest single line in the whole slate.

**They told you the answer themselves** (coach-facing email, boldness 2)
Built almost entirely from quoted forum language: skeptics saying riders do not keep practising what they learned, laid directly beside the same posters proposing the fix unprompted, focus on one or two things, practise, then do follow-ups that build on it. The email points out that athletes are describing a structured remote follow-up package in their own words and closes on the economic consequence with one CTA. It absorbs analyst-5's timing peg (NICA regionals just ended, the winter gap is open now) and goes out this month, with thought-leader-6 sequenced after it rather than alongside, so the list receives one off-season argument told twice, not three competing versions.
Hook: subject line, your athletes already designed your remote package, opening with the observation that the most useful product feedback a coach will read this winter is sitting in a forum thread written by people complaining about coaching.
Why now: the source thread is live and the window it describes opened in the last two weeks.
Critics: unanimous advance at 7.3, the cleanest construction of the three off-season emails because the audience's own quoted words do the selling.

**A coaching season, itemised** (newsletter plus blog post, scoped per the operator, boldness 4)
Money Diaries applied to coaching economics: one anonymised coach's honest season ledger told week by week, the rained-out Saturdays, the 22 dollar bike park shifts, the winter zeros, published as a single newsletter edition with a blog summary under a question heading. The full series, ending with a coach carrying a remote roster through winter with down months disclosed, is earned later once two coaches willing to open their books are secured, with Rich's own coaching numbers as the fallback first ledger. This is the only idea on the slate that produces citable original data on coaching economics, which feeds the AEO flywheel directly, and producer-6's funnel carousel gets rebuilt on these real numbers rather than hypothetical arithmetic once they exist.
Hook: subject line, a coach's season, week by week, including the part where the income stops. Opening line, nobody publishes what a coaching year actually looks like in money, so one coach kept the receipts and let us print them.
Why now: instructor pay data is current, the industry crisis is this month's dominant story, and the build-in-public conversation has shifted toward exactly this kind of down-months-included disclosure.
Critics: advanced at 7.0, the only pitch producing original citable data, with the operator's condition adopted: one ledger first, the series when sourcing allows.

## Wildcards worth the risk

**Signing day for the rest of us** (Reels series, boldness 5, two kill votes, kept anyway). Deadpan transfer journalism applied to ordinary athletes committing to winter remote rosters, a 43 year old accountant from Calgary agreeing terms on a six month remote deal, plus a reusable announcement template coaches post for their own signings. The risk is real and twofold: the breathless transfer-bulletin register strains the brand's no-hype voice rules, and the audience advocate doubts amateur riders live deeply enough inside Fabrizio Romano conventions for deadpan parody to land. The defence is that the skeptic rated it 7 as the most original tonal move on the slate, the underlying claim is completely serious (pros rebuild their support systems in transfer season and amateurs should too, during a transfer window that is live right now), and the template turns athlete pride into a coach acquisition flywheel no competitor would think to build. Run it only with real consenting athletes, keep the register dry rather than breathless, and if episode one dies quietly, the sunk cost is one afternoon.

**The year nobody won, scored anyway** (Shorts series, boldness 4, two kill votes, kept conditionally). Rescoring the nine completed runs from the wind-cancelled 25th anniversary Rampage with telemetry-style skeletal overlay, building to a verdict on who the champion should have been. The risk is blunt: Rich does not own Red Bull broadcast footage, overlaid rescoring is not obviously fair-use commentary, and reuploaded footage is exactly what the October Shorts update suppresses. But the skeptic saw what the kill votes discounted, which is that the visual language is genuinely fresh and the controversy still has heat, and judging a run on video is the closest thing to a mass-audience demonstration of the trained eye the brand sells. The condition is absolute. It ships only if the rights question gets a real answer, through licensed or creator-permitted footage or a drastically reduced fair-dealing commentary cut. If that answer does not come within two weeks, the heat is gone and it dies without regret.

**The road pros about to invade XCO will prove fitness is not skill** (Shorts format, boldness 3, one kill vote, kept in rebuilt form). The 2027 UCI points rule pulling road engines into World Cup XCO is the sport's cleanest natural experiment that fitness and technique are separate trainable systems, which is the conceptual ground a technical coach needs athletes to stand on. The critics are right that the experiment has not run, World Cup footage is rights-locked, and every episode risks being a generic explainer wearing a news peg. The upside worth holding onto is the thesis, so the rebuilt version uses the UCI rule as a one-episode framing device only, then makes the argument the audience advocate asked for, on footage of actual amateurs with big engines and bad lines, with the full prediction format parked until 2027 race results provide the receipts. The risk is spending the hook before the evidence exists; the reason to take it is that whoever owns the fitness-is-not-skill argument before next season owns it during next season.

## Killed, and why

Almost nothing died for being a bad idea; things died for being the same idea. The room pitched the clip review three times, the honest-math economics carousel three times, the off-season email three times and the AI-versus-coach stance four times, and in every case the kill votes landed on the copies that added reach without adding anything new, so future sessions should check the slate for internal duplication before critics have to spend their votes on it. The second pattern was ideas built on footage that does not exist or is not owned, the Rampage broadcast series in its original form, road pros losing rock-garden time that cannot be filmed until 2027, and a transformation beat requiring three review cycles of an athlete who has not been recruited yet, which argues for a standing question at pitch time: whose camera, whose consent, does the clip exist today. Third, formats imported with their register intact (the breathless transfer bulletin) collided with the brand's voice rules, a reminder that the structure of a stolen format transfers but its tone must not. Finally, producer-3's Reel-plus-48-hour-carousel pairing was not killed but reclassified, adopted as standing publishing process for everything on this slate rather than occupying an idea slot, which is where publishing mechanics belong.

## If you only make three things this month

1. **Stop treating remote coaching as the discount version of you** (email), because it is the highest-scored piece in the room, the window it names is open right now, and one essay to a warm list is the fastest path from this report to revenue conversations.
2. **The clip review** (Instagram flagship), because the merged series is the product demonstrated weekly without ever being a demo, and seeding the first month from existing footage means it can start this week.
3. **How to film yourself so a coach can actually see something** (YouTube Short plus companion carousel), because it removes the one documented doorway objection that sits in front of every other idea on this slate, and everything downstream converts better once riders know how to send a usable clip.
