# Content Think Tank: sportsense (October 20, 2026)

The racing calendar is finished and the off-season has started, which is the structural fact underneath everything in this slate. Lake Placid closed the World Cup season on October 3 and 4 with 19-year-old Asa Vermette becoming the youngest ever elite downhill overall champion, and Rampage week delivered both Robin Goomes' historic third consecutive title on October 9 and an unprecedented weather cancellation of the men's final on October 10 that is still being argued about across Pinkbike, Vital and Singletrack. On the business side, Garmin's July 22 acquisition of TrainingPeaks still has no announced integration roadmap, the bike industry glut is in its third year with layoffs at Canyon and JobRad, and 2026 skills clinics across North America sold out with waitlists, so demand for coaching visibly exceeded in-person supply all season. The platform picture favors what sportsense can make natively: Instagram now classifies accounts by one clear topic, Reels still win reach under 50K followers while carousels earn roughly nine times the saves of single images, YouTube's September Shorts change rewards fresh question-titled uploads, and the telestrated coach-breakdown format that built large golf and lifting audiences remains completely unoccupied in MTB. Riders, meanwhile, are asking the brand's core questions in their own words in threads under a month old: whether one coaching day or sessions over months works better, and why clinic skills fade within three weeks.

## The slate

### Blog

**The remote coaching price index, or what an hour of a coach's eyes costs in every sport except ours** (original benchmark study, boldness 4)
A named, specced comparison of what athletes pay for remote video coaching across sports in 2026 (V1 Golf memberships near two thousand dollars a year, Strava plus Runna at 149.99, Hyrox gyms at 130 a month) set against documented in-person MTB rates, leading with a direct self-contained answer to what a remote MTB coaching tier should cost. It names every competitor and tradeoff honestly, including OnForm's unreliable sync compare and the unresolved Garmin question, and the claim that MTB has no remote habit is softened to only what the research can document, since one counterexample unravels a benchmark. This absorbs the rate data and forum quotes from the pricing carousel pitches, which fold in as episodes of the answer-sheet format below.
Hook: a golfer will pay up to two thousand dollars a year for a coach to review their swing remotely, a runner pays Strava 150 for a training plan, and nobody has published the price list for mountain biking, so here it is.
Why now: benchmark studies comparing named products earn roughly 3.3 times more AI citations than ordinary pages, and the question currently has no citable answer anywhere.
Critics: the highest-scored idea in the room, called the one true original artifact on the slate and the page everything else links back to.

**Can you coach mountain bike skills remotely? An honest comparison of TrainingPeaks, OnForm, and a two-way workflow** (AEO direct-answer post, boldness 1)
Opens with the self-contained answer (yes, if the feedback loop is two-way and video-based), then names what each default tool genuinely does well and where it stops: TrainingPeaks for interval files rather than technique, OnForm with its hit-or-miss sync and zero MTB structure, one-way LMS platforms. It owns the competitor comparison alone; the Garmin analysis below links to it rather than repeating it, and the OnForm and Garmin claims get re-verified the week it ships.
Hook: one search summary this month concluded that no remote mountain bike coaching platform doing skills video review exists, which means coaches asking the question are getting answers about indoor trainers instead.
Why now: 2026 buyer's guides surface no MTB skills platform at all, and coaches on the dominant platform are uncertain right now.
Critics: advanced unanimously as the AEO workhorse, with the warning that the competitor tradeoffs must stay brutally specific or it reads as a disguised pitch.

**The skills clinic is the unfinished first half of coaching delivery** (stake-in-the-ground essay, boldness 4)
The industry's foundational assumption is that the clinic is the product, and riders are saying otherwise in their own words right now ('you quickly lose the ability and confidence to do them'). The position: skill decay after a clinic is a delivery model gap rather than a coaching failure, because motor learning needs spaced feedback over weeks and the clinic compresses it into one day; the clinic's real job is the moment an athlete decides this coach is worth paying, and the product is the following six weeks. Reframed per the room so the clinic is the strong first half of delivery rather than the villain, since Rich runs a clinic business himself, and it absorbs the gentler companion piece on the same forum quote. This merges insider-2 into the stronger version.
Hook: a rider on Singletrack described your clinic perfectly this month, brilliant day, learnt loads, and three weeks later the skills were gone because there was nowhere to practice and nobody watching, and the uncomfortable question is why we keep calling that a satisfied customer.
Why now: the decay complaint appeared in near-exact words in a late-September thread, at the exact moment coaches decide how to keep clinic clients through winter.
Critics: the only blog with a position coaches will actually argue about, provided the headline stops insulting the thing the reader currently sells.

**Your athletes are already asking ChatGPT between sessions, and it is confidently wrong about the part that matters** (blog anchor plus companion Reel, boldness 3)
A two-front piece that refuses both the anti-AI rant and the AI-makes-coaching-free hype: AI is genuinely good at structure, scheduling and summarising, and systematically wrong at technique because it has never seen your athlete ride, so a skeleton overlay with nobody behind it is measurement, not coaching. Ships as one package with the raw talking-head Reel (merged in from insider-3, whose cold open carries the clip): 'somewhere between your last session and the next one, your athlete asked ChatGPT whether your advice was right, that already happened, and the question is what it said back.'
Hook: a rider on MTBR admitted he runs his coach's advice past ChatGPT after every session, and before you get angry about that, you should know the chatbot is losing, just not for the reason you think.
Why now: AI self-coaching is a live contested conversation across MTBR, Slowtwitch and cycling media this month, with documented confidently-wrong technique advice.
Critics: the most defensible take in the AI cluster, strongest produced as one unit of blog anchor plus DM-able clip rather than two projects.

**What 'get your weight back' actually means, measured** (myth audit with angle-measured stills, boldness 3)
The sport's most contradictory piece of advice is still being taught in currently published guides even though veteran instructors called it outdated years ago, so answer engines actively cite contradiction today. Per the room's fix, the angle-measured comparison stills of a genuinely rearward rider versus a centered one on the same steep section are the artifact, with the prose doing the work of captions: the direct answer up top, exactly when hips travel back and when they must not, and an honest account of why the myth once protected riders. The stills slice into a carousel, and the piece needs matched footage confirmed before it gets scheduled.
Hook: two riders can both be told to get their weight back on a steep section, and one will ride it better while the other loses the front wheel, because the advice is not describing a position, it is mispronouncing one.
Why now: a documented contradiction in live published advice is the single best setup for a citable page.
Critics: the measurements are the novelty, so lead with the data nobody has published rather than the essay that has been written for a decade.

### YouTube

**One question, one mechanism: the overlay breakdown series, starting with 'why do I get bucked'** (recurring Shorts republished as Reels, boldness 2)
The consolidated version of the idea the room pitched three separate times, which is proof of how obvious the whitespace is: each episode takes one perennial question in the exact phrasing riders type ('why do I get bucked', 'what causes a dead sailor', 'how do I get my confidence back after a crash'), opens cold on a skeletal overlay mid-draw over real rider footage, names the mechanism, and shows the correction with angle measurement on screen. It absorbs the forum-verbatim episode bank and the question-as-literal-title Shorts strategy from the duplicate pitches, runs fortnightly rather than weekly so one founder can sustain it, and every episode is a demo of structured video feedback without a pitch. This merges insider-4 and producer-2 into analyst-4.
Hook: cold open on the overlay already drawing across a rider mid-buck, with the spoken line 'riders keep asking why the bike kicks them forward on jumps, and the honest answer is that fear makes you stiff at exactly the moment the bike needs you loose.'
Why now: telestrated coach breakdowns built large golf and lifting audiences and no MTB coach occupies the format, while September's Shorts change rewards exactly this fresh, question-titled cadence.
Critics: called the strongest repeatable engine in the slate, with consolidation of the duplicates as the condition.

**I let ChatGPT coach this drop and drew what would actually happen** (pilot Shorts teardown, boldness 5)
Rich feeds a real rider question to ChatGPT on camera, then runs the AI's confident answer through skeletal overlay and angle measurement on actual footage to show frame by frame where that advice puts the rider on the ground, while honestly crediting what the AI gets right about structure and progression. Per the operator's constraint it ships as one or two pilot episodes feeding the AI blog anchor, and only becomes a series if the pilots travel, since each episode needs footage that happens to demonstrate the specific bad advice.
Hook: split screen with ChatGPT's answer typing itself beside the rider's clip, spoken over it, 'ChatGPT just told this rider to get their weight back on the steeps, and I am going to show you frame by frame why following that advice is how you go over the bars.'
Why now: the AI self-coaching debate is live this month and nobody in any coaching niche is stress-testing AI advice with biomechanical overlay.
Critics: a saturated genre saved by an actual twist nobody else holds, and the strongest product demonstration on the slate.

### Instagram

**What the coach sees (split-screen reveal series)** (recurring Reels under 90 seconds, boldness 3)
The flagship: the top half plays a submitted rider clip at full speed where everything looks fine, the bottom half plays the identical clip with skeletal overlay and angle measurement drawing in real time, and each episode ends with the one correction the coach would send back. It absorbs the Trial Reels testing discipline from its duplicate (two alternate openings tested on non-followers before each ships) and the 'learn to read a hip angle' vocabulary from the F1 pitch as recurring language rather than a separate series, and it needs a steady pipeline of submitted clips with publish permission. This merges analyst-6 and the salvageable half of maverick-1 into producer-1.
Hook: on-screen text over the already-drawing overlay, 'this run looks fine at full speed, and the bottom half of the screen is why her coach disagrees.'
Why now: the split-screen reveal is a named trending Instagram format as of October 14, the overlay grammar is now instantly legible thanks to consumer apps, and the niche is vacant.
Critics: the whole brand compressed into three seconds, advanced by all three reviewers as the canonical version of the cluster.

**One big day or small sessions over months? The spacing package** (Reel plus companion carousel, boldness 2)
One coordinated pair built from the late-September Singletrack thread where a rider who is nearly 56 asks the brand's core question verbatim: the sub-three-minute talking-head Reel ships first (republished to Shorts under the literal question title), the save-engineered carousel follows a week later with the question quoted on slide one and a 150-word AEO caption. The answer is mechanism rather than opinion, skill acquisition needs spaced feedback, and the honest conclusion is that format matters less than whether feedback continues between rides. This merges analyst-3 and insider-1 into one package.
Hook: slide one, 'I'm nearly 56. Is one big coaching day worth it, or is a series of sessions over a few months better?' A rider asked this three weeks ago, and the replies argued about coaches when they should have argued about spacing.
Why now: the thread is under a month old, right as riders plan winter progression goals.
Critics: the sharpest observation in the slate lives here (the replies argued about the wrong thing), with the instruction to run one coordinated pair rather than two competing ideas.

**The answer sheet** (recurring carousel system, boldness 2)
The operating template the standalone carousel pitches were actually episodes of: each installment is a complete reference answering one business question coaches type, engineered for saves, Google indexing of the caption, and AI quotability, with a plain screenshot-ready summary card as the final slide because sends per reach is the ranking signal a niche B2B account can win. Episode one is the plain-numbers economics of an hour of in-person coaching versus a month of remote review, opening on the raw rates rather than an anecdote, with the forum rider's quote moved to slide three where it lands harder. This absorbs analyst-2 as episode one and takes its launch questions from the live threads.
Hook: episode one, slide one, the current rates in large type, roughly 70 dollars for an hour of in-person one-to-one against the monthly cost of asynchronous review.
Why now: carousels earn roughly nine times the saves of single images, just got a dedicated editor in the Edits app, and the questions are sitting unanswered in threads from the last four weeks.
Critics: advanced as the system behind the carousels rather than a separate idea, with the first three questions locked before launch.

### Email

**The season just ended, and so did your athletes' reasons to book** (October newsletter lead, boldness 1)
The structural argument timed to the one four-week window it works: the elite calendar closed at Lake Placid, in-person volume drops every winter, clinic decay is documented in riders' own words, and the waitlists prove demand outran supply all season, so the essay walks a coach through packaging a winter remote block (filmed drills plus monthly review at a monthly price) as the bridge that keeps the relationship and the revenue until spring. One value point, one CTA, with a condensed founder-voice Story sequence the same week.
Hook: subject line, 'Your winter calendar is about to empty, and your athletes did not stop wanting to improve.'
Why now: the send lands in the exact decision window, while riders are publicly planning off-season coaching.
Critics: wins the October slot because the newsletter physically has one lead essay a month; the skeptic's push for harder economics is answered by borrowing numbers from the price index.

**Your waitlist is trying to pay you** (November newsletter lead plus supporting carousel, boldness 3)
The reframe of the industry's favourite vanity metric: every name on a sold-out clinic's waitlist is a rider who decided to pay for coaching, found the price acceptable, and was turned away by the calendar rather than by the coach. Slotted for November per the operator while the waitlist pages are still live, it absorbs the economics slides from the carousel version of the same argument, with the waitlist evidence anonymized and aggregated so it never reads as punching at a named operator the community admires. This merges thought-leader-4 into insider-5.
Hook: subject line, 'Your waitlist is trying to pay you.' Opening line, every name on a sold-out clinic's waitlist is a rider who decided they wanted coaching, found your price acceptable, and was turned away by the calendar rather than by you.
Why now: join-wait-list status is visible across eight-plus North American cities right now, exactly as winter calendars empty.
Critics: the best subject line in the entire slate, run as the email half of a deliberate pair rather than two accidental versions of one argument.

## Wildcards worth the risk

**Scenes from the coaching business** (sketch series, Instagram, boldness 5). Workplace sketch comedy built millions of followers on the unspoken rules of one profession and nobody has applied it to coaching in any sport, and the coach who laughs at the cancellation sketch is the coach who has heard the line. The risk is blunt: the entire bet rides on whether Rich can act, comedy is the furthest register from the brand's analytical voice, and a flat sketch damages credibility with the exact skeptical audience the brand courts. The mitigation is built in, one pilot gated by Trial Reels on non-followers, with the deadpan closing line doing the heavy lifting ('that sentence is not a cancellation, it is a pricing problem', donated from the killed duplicate) so the piece still works if the comedy only half lands. Worth it because a sketch that travels coach to coach is the warmest top of funnel on the slate and the sends-per-reach math favors it heavily.

**Rampage cancelled its final and accidentally made the best argument against outcome coaching** (Pinkbike long-form, boldness 4). A contrarian position inside a live argument in the room where it is happening: the sport is grieving the lost result when it should be examining a culture that builds a year of work on top of a single day, with Goomes' three-peat standing on the same mountain as the counter-evidence that repeatable preparation systems survive any cancelled day. The risks are real and named: the debate is already ten days old so this is a hard go/no-go this week, Pinkbike readers are the longest conversion path on the slate, and a founder with product skin lecturing riders invites a brutal comment section. The Natasha Bradley crash reference gets cut entirely rather than softened. Worth it because it is the least feed-invisible idea in the room and authority with the broader MTB audience compounds into authority with coaches.

**Case file 001: the athlete who vanished after her best session** (newsletter segment, boldness 4). The evidence-timeline anatomy of client churn (last booking September 28, clinic three weeks prior, nothing on the calendar after) forces an analytical specificity the standard churn essay never reaches, and the dry forensic register genuinely suits Rich. The risks: the true-crime costume can smell contrived to exactly the skeptical coach it targets, near-verbatim forum quotes in a fictional investigation would read as mining real people, and a monthly franchise could collapse into the same sales ending by case file three. So it pilots once as a one-off essay with paraphrased quotes and a lighter touch on the noir styling, and earns the franchise only if the first one lands. Worth it because no coaching newsletter in any sport is written this way.

**This corner, loading** (progress-bar progression Reel, boldness 4). The trending loading-bar edit made honest by one rule, every percentage jump must be captioned with the specific named mechanical correction that caused it, never courage or motivation, which quietly dramatizes the entire remote-tier argument in the format itself. The risk is that it is undeliverable as timed content: it needs weeks of dated footage of one athlete on one skill, which may not exist yet, so the Rampage-week hook will be gone before the asset exists. The move is to start the footage pipeline now, either an archived past progression or a capture agreement with one current athlete, and ship it in winter as proof rather than chasing this month's trend report. Worth it because it is the rare piece that works on athletes and coaches simultaneously. (Merges producer-3's serialized same-skill structure into maverick-6's format.)

## Killed, and why

The room's corpses share three causes. First, self-duplication: the overlay question-and-answer series was pitched three times, the split-screen reveal three times, the sketch twice, the progress bar twice, and the spacing question twice, which says the research pointed hard at a few true opportunities and the discipline is consolidating into one owned version per opportunity rather than running parallel copies. Second, costumes over concepts: the F1 telemetry framing, the generic cost-breakdown wrapper, and the softer twin of the clinic essay all dressed an existing idea differently instead of adding a new argument, and each survived only as a donated line or dataset inside a stronger pitch. Third, the brand talking to itself: the hook lab died because coaches open the newsletter for help with their business, not for Rich's Instagram metrics, and because a sub-50K account's 72-hour tests produce sample sizes too thin for a mechanism-driven brand to publish as data (the testing continues privately as process, not content). Future sessions should also note the taste line the critics drew twice: the Atherton fire and the Bradley crash are real people's bad days, not rhetorical garnish.

## If you only make three things this month

1. **The remote coaching price index** (blog). The most citable artifact pitched, a few days of verifiable desk work, and the page that hands every carousel and email on this slate its numbers while becoming the answer AI engines quote when coaches ask what to charge.
2. **What the coach sees** (Instagram). The flagship recurring Reel where the product renders the bottom half of the screen, the brand argument lands in three seconds, and first-mover position in an empty niche starts compounding with episode one.
3. **The season just ended** (email, October newsletter lead). The decision window is these four weeks and then it is gone until next autumn; everything else on the slate can slip a month, this cannot.
