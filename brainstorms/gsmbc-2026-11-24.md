# Content Think Tank: GSMBC — 2026-11-24

The niche is in an off-season defined by collapse rather than racing. The UCI and Warner Bros. Discovery confirmed 2026 as the final Enduro World Cup season with team registrations down from over twenty in 2024 to five, a wave of factory XC programs folded (Cannondale three days after a World Cup win, plus BMC, Lapierre, Origine and Unno), and Ghost shut down entirely in late November after failing to find a buyer. The season's competitive stories cut the other way: 19-year-old Asa Vermette became the youngest ever DH World Cup overall champion, Robin Goomes three-peated at Rampage on October 8 while wind cancelled the men's final on October 12 with no champion crowned, and Gracey Hemstreet won the first Hardline BC on home soil on October 16. On the format side, Metricool's and Socialinsider's 2026 benchmarks show carousels beating Reels for established accounts, Google's November 2026 core update is rolling out while publisher search traffic sits down roughly a third year over year, and public pay data current to July 2026 puts the average US instructor at 23.63 dollars an hour just as BICP's convention push professionalizes the category. GSMBC was named in a Pinkbike comment thread as one of the established certification bodies, which means the external audience is already comparing credentials. The room's job was to turn that upheaval into the coaching argument, and the slate below mostly did.

## The slate

### Blog

**If the Skill Did Not Stick, the Coaching Did Not Happen** (long-form essay, boldness 4). The coaching industry judges a clinic by the day itself, breakthroughs before lunch and five stars at the car park, and this piece turns that standard against the profession, certified coaches included. Robert Bjork's work on desirable difficulties explains why performance on the day predicts almost nothing about retention, so a clinic engineered to peak on Sunday is engineered to evaporate, and the accountable coach designs the fourteen days after the session with spacing, retrieval and self-testing built in, then asks to be judged there. The piece closes with the concrete what-the-coach-hands-over framework and opens on the image of the rider rolling into the corner three weeks later as her outside foot does the old thing, both absorbed from the merged insider and analyst versions of the same idea. Hook: "A rider on Singletrack World added up what he spent on coaching, a little over 400 pounds, and concluded that his riding had not moved, and the uncomfortable part is that every one of those sessions probably went well." Why now: skill decay is the loudest documented complaint in rider forums and coaches are building 2027 clinic calendars right now. Critics called it the only retention piece with teeth, the accountability move no competitor can copy, and killed the two softer duplicates into it.

**A Certificate Is Not a Pedagogy** (long-form essay, boldness 5). A certification company publishing the argument that the credential is an entry ticket and the profession begins after it, defining what practicing an actual pedagogy requires: filming your own teaching and reviewing it the way Doug Lemov reviewed film of Michigan's Dusty May on the way to a national championship, auditing feedback habits, accumulating structured repetitions over years rather than a weekend. It converts the live credential skepticism in the Pinkbike comments into the brand's own position and never claims GSMBC meets the standard, letting the depth do that work. Hook: "Doug Lemov helped coach Michigan basketball to a national championship by filming the coach, not the players, and almost nobody in mountain bike coaching, our corner of it included, has ever watched film of themselves teaching." Why now: BICP's convention and Global Skills Day push have made certification bodies visible to riders for the first time, and the thread comparing BICP to GSMBC and PMBIA deserves an answer. All three critics advanced it as the one position competitors cannot copy without self-harm; the only coordination note is that the Lemov story gets spent once, and the coaching-transcript feature below takes a different ninety seconds.

**The App Can Hear Your Brakes. It Cannot See Your Eyes.** (long-form essay, boldness 3). AI self-coaching just arrived in the sport, with the MTBCoach sensor app promising real-time audio corrections and newsletters teaching chatbot self-coaching, and this piece attacks the conflation at the center of the trend: measurement is not diagnosis. Per the skeptic's fix, the whole essay stays at ground level, one corner, one causal chain, the sensor registering brake drag while the coach sees the eyes drop and the hips never rotate, with Richard Schmidt's guidance hypothesis carrying the mechanism and the human-versus-AI abstraction never appearing. The app stays the occasion, not the target. Hook: "The new MTBCoach app will tell you, in your earbuds, mid-descent, that you braked too long in that corner, and it will be correct, and it will have told you the least useful true thing about what just happened." Why now: the app is live on the App Store this winter, which is the moment clients start asking coaches why they should pay for what software does free. The operator called it the best effort-to-payoff ratio on the slate.

**Ask It Like a Coach** (standing blog strand, boldness 2). Every post title is a long, specific coaching-scenario question phrased the way a working coach would type it, how to coach a rider who stiffens through rock gardens, what changes when the student is on an eMTB and the group is not, with the plain answer in the first two sentences and the mechanism underneath. Merged with the analyst's duplicate, whose rock-garden scenario becomes post one. Per the skeptic's condition, each post is a coaching essay first, the first three titles are scenarios no generic MTB site would attempt, and the discoverability rationale never appears in the content. Hook, opening post one: "A rider who stiffens through rock gardens is not short on technique, they are long on threat, and the fix starts two corners before the rocks." Why now: ChatGPT prompts average 23 words against Google's three, nearly half of AI Overview searches end without a click, and answer-first pages matching one real question are what the engines cite as the November core update rolls out. Critics called it the repeatable backbone the blog lacks, measured on the honest metric of citations rather than clicks, with the caveat that nobody will feel it working for a year.

**The YouTube Coaches Are All Right, Which Is the Problem** (long-form essay, boldness 2). Built from the recurring forum complaint that video technique advice contradicts itself, the piece argues that most of the contradictory advice is correct, for a specific rider, on a specific bike, in a situation the video never specifies, and that a trained coach's actual skill is reading the context and selecting which correction matters now. Per the skeptic's rework, the spine is one contradiction pair resolved live, drop your heels versus stay light, showing exactly which rider and terrain makes each true, and the tone stays generous to the YouTubers throughout. Hook: "One video told him to drop his heels, another told him to stay light on his feet, and both presenters ride better than he ever will, so now he is sessioning a rock garden while trying to obey two instructions his body cannot do at once." Why now: the complaint is circulating on BikeRadar and MTBR at the same moment AI apps promise automated answers to contextual questions. Critics liked that it is built from the rider's lived confusion and quietly answers the app threat without naming it.

### Pinkbike

**The Race Series Was Never the Point** (feature, boldness 4). The highest-scored idea in the room. The whole industry is writing the same obituary, the Enduro World Cup dead, five factory programs folded, Ghost gone, therefore the development pipeline is broken, and this piece attacks the assumption: factory teams rarely build riders, they harvest riders built somewhere else. Vermette was produced by Durango's terrain, racing volume and local coaching long before a factory truck arrived, Isla Short walked away to build her own program, and Rissveds and Blunk describe careers run on self-directed process. Teams are distribution, coaching is production, and the sport just learned which one it can lose, with Christian Textor's "more of an opportunity than a tragedy" as the pull quote. Hook: "In six weeks this autumn the Enduro World Cup got a death date, Cannondale folded its race program three days after a World Cup win, and Ghost closed its doors for good, and nearly every obituary made the same mistake about what the sport actually lost." Why now: the closures are the biggest story of the off-season and no outlet has named the durable infrastructure. All three critics advanced it at 8 or 9, with one hard condition: every athlete claim and death date verified against current reporting before it ships.

**What Makes This Coaching Great** (feature, boldness 4). Rick Beato's stem-isolation format crossed with Lemov's film-the-coach practice: ninety seconds of real trailside coaching printed verbatim and annotated line by line, the question choice, the demonstration, the four seconds of silence, the timing of the one correction that mattered, closing with a self-review protocol any coach can run on a phone recording. MTB media has filmed riders for thirty years and never treated the coach's performance as the subject. The raw material sits in Rich's own course footage, which makes it rare among bold pitches, high concept and low logistics. Hook: "Here is a transcript of ninety seconds of coaching, word for word, including four seconds of silence that did more work than anything said out loud, and by the end of this article you will be able to say exactly why." Why now: Teach Like a Champion now sells film-the-coach review to basketball programs, and Outside's editorial cuts have thinned Pinkbike's bench exactly when contributed depth is scarce. Critics advanced it unanimously, with the note to secure the recording before committing the slot and to let the certification essay own the Lemov anecdote.

**Nine Months, a New Bike, and Still Scared: Rebuilding a Rider After the Crash** (feature, boldness 2). A sports psychology piece built from the rawest recurring forum thread, the rider whose recovery took nine months and a bike change, the one whose confidence stayed gone for years, and the folk remedies swapped with no framework underneath. Per the skeptic's fix, the week-by-week return protocol is the spine rather than garnish, with fear reconditioning and graded exposure explained as practice design, firmly inside coaching scope rather than clinical treatment, and post-crash caution reframed as information rather than weakness. Hook: "He has ridden past the corner forty times since the crash, his bike is newer, his protection is better, his fitness is back, and his hands still go numb on the brakes thirty feet before it, which tells you the injury that matters was never to his collarbone." Why now: the threads are live and the off-season is when riders sit with the crash they have not processed. Critics called it the highest emotional resonance on the Pinkbike slate, the piece riders send to the friend who quit.

**What Your Fastest Friend Can Teach You, and What He Cannot** (feature, boldness 2). The winner of a four-way pileup, merged per the operator's instruction. It concedes the opposing argument properly, Albert Bandura on observational learning explaining why a faster friend reliably improves line choice, pace tolerance and commitment, then draws the line the argument never draws: a friend sees outcomes, not causes, and cannot sequence or withhold feedback, which is where Carolee Winstein and Richard Schmidt showed motor learning actually turns. It absorbs the insider duplicate's practical closing map of when to chase friends and when the money is worth it, and if its comment section produces the next good fight, that fight gets pitched as its own one-off, which quietly keeps the Comment Section Clinic engine without promising Pinkbike a column. Hook: "You follow his line through the rock garden, you match his braking for as long as you can hold his wheel, and by October you are measurably better at exactly one thing, which is following him." Why now: the debate ran live in the BICP Global Skills Day comments where GSMBC was named, and the March 28, 2026 event cycle will restart it. Critics judged it the most generous and best-researched of the four copies and killed the rest into it.

### Instagram

**One Thing the Coach Saw** (standing monthly carousel series, boldness 1). A second monthly slot beside the course-schedule post, each installment distilling one coaching observation from that month's blog into a seven-slide arc, one concrete claim on slide one, the behavior shown then explained, a save ask on the final slide, with the caption written as a 150-word mini-article now that Google indexes professional-account captions. Built once as a locked Canva template so it ships in an hour a month. Merged with the analyst duplicate, whose rear-brake slide one ("a rider who drags the rear brake through every corner is telling you where their eyes stopped") joins the installment bank. Hook, slide one: "Watch a rider's outside heel in the three seconds before a rock garden, because it will tell you whether they trust their brakes before their mouth does." Why now: carousels beat Reels in both 2026 benchmark studies, Mosseri says sends per reach is the growth metric and unswiped carousels get re-served, so the carousel-only account should double down rather than diversify. Critics called it the cheapest compounding asset pitched.

**Start Small Is Half an Answer** (carousel, installment one of the series, boldness 3). Built from the adult-learning-to-jump threads, the claim is that repetition on the comfortable size builds confidence in that jump rather than the skill of jumping, and transfer requires planned variation in speed, lip shape and takeoff, closing with a fear-managed sequence and a named decision point for moving up. The slightly contestable reps claim is softened just enough to survive the comments it will attract, which in this case are fuel. Hook, slide one: "The rider who has hit the same four-foot tabletop two hundred times is not two hundred reps closer to the six-footer, and here is what the start small advice leaves out." Why now: the MTBR thread is the community's live reference for adult jump fear, and this is precisely the carousel one coach forwards to another. Critics advanced it as exactly what the second slot should contain, buildable in an afternoon.

**Nine Riders Dropped In. Would You Have?** (carousel, later installment of the series, boldness 5). A decision-game carousel rebuilt from the cancelled Rampage men's final, each slide one real condition read from October 12 with a commit-or-hold decision before the swipe, revealing that condition reading is coachable judgment rather than a vibe. Per the operator's rework it launches inside the standing series rather than as a third posting commitment, every condition detail verified from event coverage, and the tone held as judgment training rather than second-guessing riders who held. Hook, slide one: "You are rider ten on the start ledge at Kolob Terrace, and the wind just gusted hard enough to move your bike against your leg, so decide before you swipe." Why now: the cancelled final is the most public go-or-hold decision of the year. Critics called commit-before-swipe the rare carousel twist that is actually a twist; nobody in MTB coaching builds decision games.

### Email

**Hand Histories: You Hold the Clipboard** (recurring send pair, boldness 4). The poker hand-history format imported into coaching: a Thursday send presents one real scenario in clipped notation up to the moment the coach must act, stops, and asks for the reader's call by reply, and Monday opens with the best replies before working through the mechanism, built on the prediction-before-feedback logic poker study culture now runs on. Per the operator's fix, it pilots as a monthly pair rather than claiming both weekly sends, and Monday's reveal is written to stand alone on the research even if three people answer. Over a season it compounds into a library of solved scenarios no other body in MTB owns, and the replies are the engagement signal that now feeds deliverability. Hook: "Third lap, your rider brakes at the same spot above the rock garden, shoulders up, elbows locked, and tells you it felt better that time, and you have thirty seconds before they roll in again, so reply with what you say, and only what you say." Why now: reply-and-click engagement is the 2026 deliverability firewall, and the format and the metric point the same direction. Two critics advanced it as the one email idea with a structural twist.

**Twenty-Three Dollars an Hour Is Not a Pay Problem** (Monday send, boldness 4). The winner of the four-way pay cluster. ZipRecruiter has the average US instructor at 23.63 dollars an hour, and the essay argues both standard responses, the shrug and the advice to charge more, miss the mechanism: that number is the market price of undifferentiated instruction, and the coaches who sustain a practice are selling a structurally different product, a designed outcome with retention built in, where the differentiator is pedagogy rather than marketing. It absorbs the insider duplicate's named behaviors (session continuity, a defined specialty, charging for diagnosis rather than hours) as the middle section, and its reply prompt, asking readers what they charge and what the client is actually buying, doubles as recruitment for the survey below. Hook: "ZipRecruiter puts the average mountain bike instructor at 23.63 dollars an hour, which is less than the hourly rate of the shuttle driver who drops their clients at the trailhead, and the industry's explanation for this is wrong." Why now: the pay data is current to July 2026 and sits awkwardly beside the category's professionalization push. Critics scored it the sharpest of four pay pitches because it has a thesis instead of a number.

**The First Five Emails** (welcome sequence, boldness 2, merged from two pitches). The two welcome-sequence pitches become one build: constructed from the strongest existing Monday and Thursday essays, which is the cheaper path for one person, but restructured so each send teaches one load-bearing GSMBC idea rather than replaying greatest hits, each ending with one real coaching question the reader can reply to, with replies routed into the Ask It Like a Coach scenario bank. Hook, first send: "Somewhere in your last ten sessions there was a rider you could not figure out, and by the end of this email I want to know who it was." Why now: welcome emails earn 83 percent opens, the highest-engagement real estate GSMBC owns, and it is currently empty while deliverability now runs on engagement. Each version drew kill votes as a duplicate of the other; the merge takes the operator-preferred construction and the stronger spine, which is the version every critic described wanting.

## Wildcards worth the risk

**Cannondale Factory Racing, Dead at 32** (Pinkbike feature, boldness 5, one kill vote). Formal Economist-style obituaries for the institutions MTB lost this autumn, with the heirs named at the end, and no MTB outlet has ever run an obituary for a team. The risk is double: the literary form collapses into gimmick at anything under full execution, and the closing argument is the same thesis as The Race Series Was Never the Point, so run together they cannibalize each other. The upside is that it is the single most screenshottable thing the room produced. The call: ship the straight argument first, and if it lands, run the obituary page two weeks later as the formal echo, with the heirs section cut to one line so the two pieces rhyme instead of repeating, and every death date verified. If the execution cannot be flawless, donate the opening device to the straight piece and walk away.

**The State of MTB Coaching survey** (blog data piece plus Pinkbike feature, boldness 5, merged from three pitches). A short annual survey of working coaches, rates, income share, what they were never taught, published as a named year-one report, with the pay email above recruiting respondents and the maverick's levels.fyi framing as the launch essay. The risk is the sample: a definitive-sounding report built on forty responses torches the consultant positioning it exists to build. It is worth taking because primary research is the one asset in this niche nobody can copy, it earns roughly triple the AI citations of ordinary pages, and it compounds every year it runs. The mitigation is baked in: run the survey through the newsletter and partner channels first, publish the n transparently, and brand year one as a baseline rather than the state of anything.

**Silly Season Stuff: How a Bike Team Actually Dies** (one Monday send, boldness 4, two kill votes, defended here in reduced form). The critics were right that a standing Matt Levine pastiche is a voice the brand cannot sustain and a reporting burden one founder cannot carry, so the strand dies. The single email survives: one mechanism explainer tracing how Ghost actually died, from stalled bike sales through sponsorship line items to the junior whose contract evaporated, landing on who absorbs the development work, sent the week the Pinkbike feature runs as its email companion. The risk is that industry-finance territory is a credibility stretch for a coaching body. It is worth one send because nobody in MTB media explains the plumbing, and the audience is living inside this news cycle right now, which will not come again.

## Killed, and why

Almost nothing died for being a bad idea; things died for being the same idea. The room pitched the faster-friend rebuttal four times, the clinic-decay piece three times, the 23.63 number four times, the coaching survey three times, and the second carousel slot and the welcome sequence twice each, which means the strongest research findings acted as magnets and the personas converged instead of diverging, so the critics spent most of their votes running tiebreaks rather than judging concepts. The lesson for future sessions is to assign findings to personas rather than letting everyone draft off the same five. The second pattern was borrowed costumes: the Levine newsletter voice and the Economist obituary form both drew kills because a voice import the brand guide does not permit is a liability no matter how good the source material, and the surviving wildcards keep the mechanism while dropping the impersonation. The third was strategy showing through the content, the SEO-first strand pitched as an SEO play and hooks written in the staccato fragments the writing rules ban; the versions that advanced made the same moves invisibly, which is the difference between using the trend research and reciting it.

## If you only make three things this month

**The Race Series Was Never the Point** for Pinkbike, because the closures are the story of the off-season and the piece loses its moment if it waits past the news cycle; verify every claim, then ship it this week. **If the Skill Did Not Stick, the Coaching Did Not Happen** for the blog, because it answers the market's loudest objection with the accountability move no competitor can afford to copy, and coaches are planning 2027 calendars now. **One Thing the Coach Saw** on Instagram, launched with the Start Small installment, because it is an hour a month on a locked template, it compounds, and it turns every blog the other two picks represent into a second asset for free.
